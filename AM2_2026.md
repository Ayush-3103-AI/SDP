Abstract
This paper introduces a closed-loop defect detection/correction system for
Marlin-based fused deposition modeling (FDM) printers. Real-time defect
detection will be accomplished through a computer vision pipeline using
YOLO (you only look once) as the object detection algorithm, which will
identify common printing defects including stringing, cracking, underextrusion, and layer collapse failure while in progress. Furthermore, a multilevel confidence filter will enhance system robustness by ensuring that only
sufficiently confident and sufficiently time-cohesive defect detections
trigger corrective action. Another problem addressed by this work is the
unintended printer reset issue related to the Data Terminal Ready (DTR)
signal behaviour of Creality-class mainboards. To maintain a reliable and
stable serial communications channel, this work establishes a continuity in
serial communication that supports the printer without accidental drops in
communication or reset. When defects have been identified, G-code is
dynamically created by this framework to rewrite print parameters (for
example, extrusion flow rate and temperature settings). This framework has
been created with commercially available components and firmware, and
therefore, provides an economically viable and reliable means for enhancing
the reliability and the autonomic operation of an FDM printer during
production.
1. Introduction
Over the years, Additive Manufacturing (AM) has dramatically evolved, with Fused
Deposition Modeling (FDM) becoming the leading technology for AM. By switching to
FDM, you can choose any material for the project and it will be less expensive comparatively
and easier to work with. Because of these three qualities, it has gained widespread acceptance
among professionals, researchers and engineers. However, FDM is not flawless. However,
despite its developments and growth, there are still many challenges for FDM with respect
to achieving consistent part quality and repeatable processes. Traditional techniques and

processes for AM must be controlled with precision in order to achieve the desired results
for dimensional accuracy, finish quality, and mechanical strength. However, variation is
unavoidably introduced by the layer-by-layer process of FDM. It is common for 3D printing
to experience issues with under-extrusion, warping, layer misalignment, stringing, nozzle
jams, and total print failure. These problems only become worse when long print times are
required to create complicated prints, resulting in wasted materials and significant delays. All
of these issues lead to a dramatic decrease in productivity.
The limitations mentioned above are very important in fields like aerospace, automotive,
medical engineering, and precision tooling where mistakes are not allowed. The Industry4.0
approach replaces traditional manufacturing processes that use people with systems that can
manage their own operations, make smart and quick decisions, and make real-time
corrections without any help from people.
In the last few years, scientists have begun to incorporate AI, deep learning, and computer
vision in FDM.  Vision-based tools, such as convolutional neural networks and lightweight
object detection models, have the ability to detect surface defects, like poor filament lines or
uneven layers, immediately upon their occurrence.  Early detection helps avert the
development of small faults into major failures. In cases where the defects are not visible, the
experts are exploring the use of thermal imaging cameras, acoustic emission analysis,
environmental sensing, and data integration from various sources. Besides, digital twins and
centralized monitoring for entire fleets of printers are some of the ideas aiming to
automatically resolve and predict issues at a larger scale. Even though the latter innovations
increase the rate of detecting defects, most of the existing systems passively monitor for
problems and seldom intervene to remedy them during the process. Closed-loop control,
where a printer not only identifies a defect but also eliminates it during its operation, is still
not well developed, particularly in the case of consumer-grade printers. The available
solutions mostly involve making changes to the print settings through G-code alteration but
these often presuppose the ideal hardware setup. There is a gap between what is theoretically
feasible and what actually functions in a person's garage or lab because the majority of FDM
printers have practical limitations. One of the many hardware problems that FDM printers
encounter is particularly noteworthy: many FDM printers run on Marlin firmware, and when
they are connected via USB, the Data Terminal Ready (DTR) signal activates the
microcontroller and compels it to halt transmission. In sectors like aerospace, automotive,
medical engineering, and precision tooling, where anything less than perfection is just
unacceptable, the aforementioned limitations are crucial.
2. Literature Survey
FDM has driven rapid growth since its early introduction in the 1990s in AM [1]. Despite its
widespread use, reliability and repeatability remain problematic, especially in industrial
settings. Initial studies showed that components produced by printing may face potential
dimensional inaccuracies, poor surface quality, and lower mechanical strength due to lax
process control conditions [2]. The foundation for comprehending material behavior and
failure mechanisms in AM processes was laid by these works. As Industry 4.0 has emerged,
research has become more focused on FDM system optimization and intelligent monitoring.
According to recent analyses, research on smart manufacturing technologies for polymer
extrusion has significantly increased, indicating a shift toward automation, machine learning,
and real-time data analytics [3]. This change is part of a larger attempt to use computational
techniques to control the intrinsic variability of the FDM process. One important area of
research is optimizing G-code based on data. Machine learning techniques are used to change
the settings for extrusion and temperature during printing instead of relying on fixed process
parameters. Initial demonstrations of learning-based G-code optimization exhibited

quantifiable enhancements in print quality via adaptive control [4]. Further research has
delved into the detection of abnormalities in FDM through the application of convolutional
neural networks. The results revealed that the feature extraction based on deep learning is
capable of spotting defects like under-extrusion and irregular bead geometry in a very
dependable way [5]. Nevertheless, these methods have mostly been concerned with the
detection of defects and thus do not go on to taking real-time corrective action, therefore the
area of closed-loop control is not fully dealt with. Also, the use of vision-based in-situ
monitoring systems has been gaining popularity. Multi-camera frameworks combined with
supervisory logic have been proposed to detect anomalies during printing in real time [6].
Although these systems are good at finding flaws, they frequently ignore the firmware and
hardware limitations of consumer-grade printers. Extensive analyses of surface monitoring
methods, including optical, laser-based, and multi-sensor approaches, show consistent
advancements in deep learning-based defect detection, but also point out the lack of reliable
in-situ correction mechanisms [7]. Firmware restrictions and communication latency
continue to be major obstacles to real-time control on inexpensive printers, according to
broader discussions on AI-driven automation [8].
Defects like stringing and material blobs during printing were successfully detected by
early real-time defect detection systems that used high-resolution vision [9]. Nevertheless,
these systems were only capable of monitoring and did not allow for remedial action. The
motivation for developing multi-modal sensing to improve quality assessment is obtained
from the various reviews on full-field measurement techniques, showing that defect
signatures appear in both optical and thermal domains [10]. Multi-sensor fusion, which uses
the integration of thermal, vibrational, and visual data through hybrid deep learning models,
has meanwhile been proposed to increase robustness [11]. These systems increase the
accuracy of anomaly detection by using redundant sensors, but they frequently need
specialized hardware, which restricts their use on inexpensive FDM platforms. Large
language models have been used in more recent frameworks to analyze sensor data and
automatically produce corrective G-code [12]. Although conceptually novel, these methods
rely significantly on large computational resources and fail to sufficiently address
communication problems at the firmware level that interfere with continuous control. For
real-time defect monitoring in AM, deep learning-based object detection models have been
used, and they have demonstrated high accuracy for common defects like stringing and poor
extrusion [13]. Similar studies show that machine learning methods are significantly better
at surface defect detection than traditional image-processing methods [14]. Moreover, some
predictive models developed from historical data were also suggested to detect defects in
their early stages, which supports preventive quality control rather than real-time remediation
[15]. Alternative sensing techniques have been investigated in addition to vision-based
approaches. It has been demonstrated that acoustic emission monitoring can identify
mechanical anomalies that are not visible, such as motor faults, belt misalignment, and nozzle
vibration [16]. The general applicability of data-driven monitoring across AM technologies
has been demonstrated by the successful application of machine learning-based defect
detection to metallic additive manufacturing processes [17]. Computer vision-based remote
real-time defect detection systems have been proposed to detect stringing during FDM
printing, but their correction logic is still limited and not sufficiently validated [18]. Print
quality has also been found to be influenced by environmental factors. Print variability and
failure modes can be better understood by keeping an eye on variables like temperature,
humidity, and air composition [19]. Improved defect detection capabilities have also been
shown by hybrid sensing techniques that integrate thermal and visual data, providing insights
that go beyond their initial metallic AM focus [20].

But here’s the thing: if you tried to fix every little issue the moment you saw it, you’d wreck
your print. Imagine dropping the nozzle temp every time stringing pops up. You’d just
bounce between settings, and everything would go off the rails. That’s why there are
cooldown timers for every adjustment—so you can’t just keep cranking temps and flow rates
up and down without giving things a chance to settle.
